# Master Outline: The Mechanics of Civilization (42 Chapters)

## Part I: Surplus, Rules, and Stone (3500 BCE – 1200 BCE)
1. **Mud, Canals, and the First Ledgers (Sumer):** Irrigation coordination, caloric surplus, grain taxes, and cuneiform accounting.
2. **How to Feed Ten Thousand Stonecutters (Old Kingdom Egypt):** Nile flood predictability, state bakeries, copper tools, and the physics of Giza.
3. **Bronze, Tin, and Long-Distance Risk (Bronze Age Trade Network):** Why bronze required 2,000-mile supply chains; private merchant contracts in Assur (Kanesh tablets).
4. **Carving Rules in Stone (Babylon & Early Law):** Hammurabi's Code, courts, property rights, debt, and why written rules beat blood feuds.
5. **Plumbing, Weights, and Standardized Bricks (Indus Valley):** Urban grids, clean water engineering, and uniform stone weights for fair trade without royal palaces.
6. **Millet, Bronze Vessels, and Ancestor States (Shang China):** High-heat piece-mold metallurgy, oracle bones, and mobilizing labor along the Yellow River.
7. **When the Supply Chain Snapped (1177 BCE Collapse):** How tightly linked Mediterranean palaces failed together when trade, drought, and raids collided.

## Part II: Iron, Coins, and Empires (1200 BCE – 200 CE)
8. **Cheap Iron and Alphabets (Phoenicians & Iron Age):** Why common iron ore broke royal bronze monopolies; 22-letter scripts for merchants.
9. **Roads, Couriers, and Governing Strangers (Persian Empire):** Royal Road logistics, keeping local laws while collecting predictable tribute.
10. **Stamped Silver and Citizen Courts (Classical Athens):** Coinage solving the weighing problem; maritime loans, silver mines, and voting assemblies.
11. **Standard Axles, Canals, and Merit Exams (Qin & Han China):** Dujiangyan irrigation, state iron/salt monopolies, standardized weights, and bureaucratic statecraft.
12. **Concrete, Arches, and Aqueduct Gradients (Roman Engineering):** Pozzolana volcanic ash, semicircular keystone arches, and moving water by gravity over fifty miles.
13. **Grain Ships, Legions, and Roman Law (Pax Romana):** Feeding a million people in Rome from Egyptian farms; partnerships (*societates*) and contracts.
14. **Monsoon Winds and Oasis Towns (Early Silk & Maritime Roads):** Using seasonal ocean winds and camel caravans to price silk, pepper, and glass across Eurasia.

## Part III: Faiths, Guilds, and Overland Webs (200 – 1350)
15. **When Taxes Fail and Currency Thins Out (Late Rome):** Silver coin debasement, inflation, defense costs, and the shift to self-sufficient country estates.
16. **Walls, Cisterns, and Gold Bezants (Constantinople):** Triple defensive walls, underground water storage, and a currency trusted for seven centuries.
17. **Paper, Checks, and Translation Workshops (Abbasid Caliphate):** Papermaking from Samarkand, *sakk* payment notes, underground *qanats*, and algebra for trade.
18. **Rice, Grand Canals, and Paper Money (Tang & Song China):** Early-ripening rice doubling harvests, canal pound locks, movable type, and state-printed banknotes.
19. **Heavy Plows, Horse Collars, and Watermills (Medieval Europe):** Mechanical energy replacing muscle; three-field crop rotation and manor agreements.
20. **Flying Buttresses and Guild Apprentices (Gothic Cathedrals):** Redirecting roof weight outward to open glass walls; financing a 100-year build across generations.
21. **Relay Horses, Passports, and Plague Ships (Mongol Exchange & Black Death):** Continental postal stations, safe merchant passage, and how labor scarcity raised peasant wages after 1348.

## Part IV: Oceans, Printing, and Credit (1350 – 1750)
22. **Floating Gardens and Mountain Roads (Tenochtitlan & Inca Andes):** *Chinampa* wetland farming, freeze-dried potato storehouses, *quipu* knotted accounting, and labor taxes without money.
23. **Double-Entry Ledgers and Banking Families (Renaissance Florence & Venice):** Assets vs. liabilities, bills of exchange, marine insurance, and funding Brunelleschi's self-supporting dome.
24. **Lead Type and Cheap Paper (Gutenberg's Printing System):** Antimony-tin alloy molds, oil ink, and what happens when books drop 90% in price.
25. **Caravels, Compass Bearings, and Ocean Winds (Age of Sail):** Hull geometry, Atlantic wind gyres, and bypassing land tolls to reach Indian Ocean spice markets.
26. **Mountain of Silver and Global Inflation (Potosí to Manila to Ming China):** Mercury amalgamation mining, China's Single Whip silver tax, and why too much silver raised bread prices in Spain.
27. **Joint-Stock Shares and Bond Markets (Amsterdam, 1602):** Splitting shipwreck risk into tradable company shares; why low government interest rates beat bigger kingdoms.
28. **Sugar, Coerced Labor, and Atlantic Triangle Trade:** Plantation capital, human enslavement, credit chains, and the brutal economics of early global commodities.

## Part V: Coal, Steam, and Steel Systems (1750 – 1914)
29. **Why Britain Burned Rock Instead of Wood (Coal & Steam):** Flooding coal mines, Newcomen and Watt's steam engines, and breaking the land-energy ceiling.
30. **Waterwheels, Power Looms, and Factory Clocks (Textile Revolution):** Precision interchangeable parts, canal barges, and shifting from cottage weaving to timed shift work.
31. **Constitutions, Patents, and Public Debt (Modern State Institutions):** Protecting inventions by law, predictable taxation, and checks on executive power.
32. **Iron Rails, Time Zones, and Telegraph Wires (19th-Century Networks):** Shrinking continental transport costs by 85%; why train schedules forced cities to synchronize clocks.
33. **Sewers, Germ Theory, and Clean Water Pipes (Modern Cities):** London's Bazalgette intercepting sewers, filtration, and how urban life expectancy finally beat rural villages.
34. **Bessemer Steel, Elevators, and Skyscraper Frames (1880–1914):** Hanging curtain walls on steel skeletons, alternating-current electricity grids, and industrial research labs.
35. **Gold Standards, Steamships, and Imperial Raw Materials:** Undersea cables, refrigerated cargo ships, and unequal industrial empires before 1914.

## Part VI: Grids, Chips, and Modern Complexity (1914 – Present)
36. **Assembly Lines and Nitrogen from Air (Early 20th Century):** Haber-Bosch synthetic fertilizer feeding billions; moving chassis lines cutting car build times from 12 hours to 93 minutes.
37. **Bank Runs, Fiat Money, and Central Banks (1929–1971):** Fractional-reserve banking panics, deposit insurance, public works, and how money works without gold.
38. **Standard Steel Boxes (The Shipping Container, 1956):** How crane-lifted metal boxes dropped port loading costs from $5.86 a ton to 16 cents and remade global factories.
39. **Transistors, Photolithography, and Cleanrooms (Silicon Age):** Printing billion-transistor logic gates with light; global supply chains where no single country can build a chip alone.
40. **Packet Switching, Fiber Optics, and Protocols (The Internet):** Undersea glass cables, TCP/IP routing around broken nodes, and zero-marginal-cost information.
41. **Vaccines, Antibiotics, and Cold Chains (Public Health Infrastructure):** Eradicating smallpox, sterile manufacturing, and keeping medicine cold to the remotest village.
42. **The Fragile Machine (How Our World Stays Running Today):** Power grids, just-in-time logistics, institutional trust, and the open engineering problems of the next century.
