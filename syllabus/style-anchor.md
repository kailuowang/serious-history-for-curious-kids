### Sample Passage: The Canal Problem

In southern Mesopotamia, almost no rain fell from May to October. By midsummer, the flat plain between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers baked into cracked gray mud. Yet ten miles away, river water flowed past by the millions of gallons.

A single family with wooden hoes could dig a ditch fifty feet long to water their own barley patch. That kept one family alive. It did not build a city. To water five thousand acres of dry land, farmers needed a main canal three miles long and twenty feet wide, with earth banks high enough to hold back the spring flood.

Try organizing three hundred neighbors to dig a three-mile ditch without a boss. Two problems appear right away. First, the farmers whose fields sit near the river get plenty of water after the first half-mile is dug; they have little reason to keep digging in the sun for farmers two miles downstream. Second, river water carries fine mud called silt. Every spring, silt settles on the bottom of the canal and clogs it. If ten families along the middle stretch skip their turn shoveling out the mud, the water slows down for everyone past them.

Even worse, while three hundred people spend two months digging ditches, nobody is baking bread or weaving cloth for them. Someone must store tons of extra barley from last year's harvest and measure out daily bowls of grain to pay the diggers.

Out of that mud came the first city governments. Priests at the local temple already stored spare grain as gifts for the city god. Soon the temple managers were measuring land with knotted ropes, assigning canal-cleaning shifts, and handing out barley rations. To keep arguments from turning into fistfights, scribes pressed reed tips into wet clay to record who owed three days of digging and who had already been paid. A **tax** was simply the share of your harvest you handed over so the canals stayed open, the granary stayed full, and the guards kept watch.
