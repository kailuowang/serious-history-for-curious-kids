# Supplemental Reference Notes — Early Sumerian Political Order (c. 3500–2000 BCE)

Compiled from mainstream Assyriological scholarship on how political authority formed in early southern Mesopotamian city-states: Thorkild Jacobsen ("Primitive Democracy in Ancient Mesopotamia," *JNES* 1943; *The Treasures of Darkness*), Marc Van De Mieroop (*A History of the Ancient Near East*), Mario Liverani (*Uruk: The First City*), Piotr Steinkeller ("On Rulers, Priests and Sacred Marriage"), and David Graeber & David Wengrow (*The Dawn of Everything*, surveying early Mesopotamian assemblies).

## 1. What Evidence We Have—and What We Do Not Have
- **No Eyewitness Origin Story:** Early Uruk (c. 3500–3000 BCE) left large temple complexes, central grain storehouses, ration bowls, and ~5,000 accounting/word-list tablets, yet **no written narrative or eyewitness record** explaining how 40,000 residents first agreed on who got to make and enforce city rules.
- **Independent City-States:** Unlike Egypt (which unified early under a single pharaoh), Sumer consisted of independently governed city-states—such as Uruk, Ur, Lagash, and Kish—each centered on its own patron god's temple and, over time, a royal palace.
- **Later Institutional Structure (from c. 2900–2000 BCE):** Surviving inscriptions and epics show two main centers of authority—the **temple** (run by priests and managers of the city god's estate) and the **palace** (led by a king, in Sumerian called a *lugal*, literally "big man")—alongside evidence of **city assemblies** of elders and free citizens.

## 2. Main Scholarly Hypotheses for How Political Order Was Established
Because no single eyewitness text explains the very first political arrangement in early Uruk, historians treat the rise of Sumerian political order as an open detective puzzle and weigh three complementary mechanisms:
1. **Shared Religious Trust & Temple Storehouses (Religious Legitimacy):** Every Sumerian city belonged in belief to its patron god, and the temple served as the god's household. Scholars note that farmers may have accepted temple managers as neutral keepers of shared grain reserves—obeying the city's god and temple felt fairer and safer than bowing to a rival neighbor.
2. **City Assemblies & Emergency War-Leaders (Consent Turning Into Permanent Kingship):** Thorkild Jacobsen (and later scholars examining Sumerian epics such as *Gilgamesh and Agga* and neighborhood *babtum* councils) argued that early Sumerian cities consulted assemblies of elders and free citizens on major choices like war. During a severe emergency—such as a flood crisis or a border fight with a neighboring city over land or canal water—a city could choose a temporary strong leader (a *lugal*, or "big man") to give fast orders. Historians think that as rivalries between cities grew, some temporary crisis leaders kept command of armed men and storehouses permanently, turning emergency leadership into hereditary kingship. (Critics caution that the surviving poetic texts are later and fragmentary, so we cannot know how democratic early assemblies truly were.)
3. **Control of Grain Reserves, Water, and Armed Force (Material Leverage):** Once temple estates and palaces controlled central grain storehouses, key canal schedules, and armed retainers, rulers had direct material leverage to reward cooperating households with barley rations and punish households that refused shared rules or labor shifts.

## 3. Connection Between Grain Surplus and Political Power
- Political power in southern Mesopotamia rested directly on controlling enough grain surplus to feed full-time administrators, builders, craftworkers, and armed guards.
- When rising soil salinity between c. 2400 and 1700 BCE shrank harvests in the far south (as tracked by Jacobsen & Adams, *Science*, 1958), southern storehouses lost their surplus edge, weakening southern city rulers and helping political power—the ability to set and enforce rules across the river plain—shift northward toward Babylon.
