# Seed Reference Packet — Chapter 1: Southern Mesopotamia (Uruk & Ur III, c. 3500–2000 BCE)

Compiled from mainstream Assyriological scholarship: Amanda H. Podany (*Weavers, Scribes, and Kings*, 2022; *The Ancient Near East: A Very Short Introduction*, OUP), Marc Van De Mieroop (*A History of the Ancient Near East*, Wiley-Blackwell), Hans J. Nissen (*Archaic Bookkeeping*, Univ. of Chicago Press), and Robert McC. Adams (*Heartland of Cities*).

## 1. Geography, Hydrology & Caloric Surplus
- **Rainfall:** Southern Mesopotamia (alluvial plain south of modern Baghdad) receives less than 100–150 mm (4–6 inches) of rain per year—below the ~200–250 mm threshold required for dry-farming grain without irrigation (Van De Mieroop, Ch. 1; Podany VSI, Ch. 1).
- **The River Timing Problem:** Unlike the Nile (which floods gently in late summer right before autumn planting), the Tigris and Euphrates flood violently in **April–May** from Anatolian snowmelt—right when winter barley is ripening and ready for harvest. Farmers had to build protective levees/dikes to keep floodwaters *off* ripening crops in spring, and storage basins/regulator gates to release water *onto* dry soil in autumn (September–October) for planting (Adams, *Heartland of Cities*).
- **River Levees & Gravity:** Over millennia, slow-moving floodwaters dropped heavy silt right beside the riverbanks, building natural raised levees. The riverbed actually sat slightly *higher* than the surrounding plain—a huge engineering gift allowing farmers to cut a gate in the bank and let gravity carry water down feeder canals into fields, though poor drainage trapped water in low basins (Adams).
- **Yield Multipliers & Seeder Plow:** With irrigated alluvial silt and the Sumerian **seeder plow** (an ox-drawn plow fitted with a vertical funnel that dropped barley seeds evenly into the furrow instead of scattering by hand, saving ~50% of seed), barley yields reached **1:15 to 1:30** (15 to 30 grains harvested for every 1 grain planted), far above rain-fed yields of 1:5 to 1:8 (Van De Mieroop; Nissen). This surplus allowed 1 farming family to feed non-farming specialists (weavers, smiths, scribes, builders).
- **The Ecological Trap (Salinization):** River water holds dissolved mineral salts. Under intense summer heat (often exceeding 45°C / 113°F), standing irrigation water evaporated and raised the salty water table, leaving white salt crusts in the topsoil. Wheat is salt-sensitive; barley tolerates more salt. Archival temple records between c. 2400 BCE and 1700 BCE show wheat dropping from ~16% of southern grain harvests to less than 2% before gone altogether in some southern cities, shifting political power northward toward Babylon (Jacobsen & Adams, *Science*, 1958).

## 2. Labor Mobilization, Rations & Taxation
- **Uruk Scale (c. 3200–3000 BCE):** Uruk grew to roughly **250–400 hectares** with an estimated **40,000 residents**—an order of magnitude larger than earlier villages (Nissen; Podany, *Weavers*, Ch. 1).
- **The Beveled-Rim Bowl:** Archaeologists find thousands of coarse, mold-made clay bowls ("beveled-rim bowls") discarded near Uruk temples and canal works. Most cluster around standardized volumes (~0.8 to 1 liter), interpreted by Nissen and Potts as mass-produced ration bowls used to measure out daily barley grain or porridge to state/temple work crews.
- **Standardized Ur III Rations (c. 2100–2000 BCE):** Administrative tablets record monthly barley rations (*še-ba*): typically **60 *sila* (~60 liters) of barley per month** for an adult male worker (~2 liters/day), **30–40 *sila*** for adult women (such as state textile mill weavers), and **10–20 *sila*** for children, supplemented periodically with wool (4 minas / ~2 kg per year) or rations of sesame/lard oil and beer (Van De Mieroop; Podany, *Weavers*).
- **Corvée Labor (*dusu*):** Free households owed seasonal labor shifts—often **15 to 30 days per year**—dredging silt from canals, repairing flood dikes, harvesting temple estates, or molding mudbricks. Wealthier citizens eventually paid silver or hired substitutes to perform their canal shifts (Podany).

## 3. Writing, Sealing & Contracts
- **Tokens to Bullae to Tablets (3500–3100 BCE):** Before full writing, accountants tracked sheep, oil jars, and grain measures with small clay tokens sealed inside hollow clay balls (*bullae*) rolled with **cylinder seals** (carved stone cylinders rolled across wet clay as tamper-evident signatures). Soon scribes pressed token shapes directly onto flat clay cushions with a cut reed stylus—birth of **proto-cuneiform** (Nissen, *Archaic Bookkeeping*).
- **What Early Writing Actually Recorded:** Roughly **85–90% of the ~5,000 archaic tablets from Uruk** are administrative and economic records (barley receipts, beer brewing inputs, herd counts, land measurements, worker rosters); the remaining ~10–15% are **lexical lists** (standardized training word-lists of professions, tools, trees, and cities copied by student scribes to keep the system uniform across cities) (Nissen). Literature and royal hymns came centuries later.
- **Concrete Artifact — The Kushim Tablet (c. 3100 BCE, Uruk III):** One famous tablet records **"29,086 measures of barley received over 37 months, signed Kushim"**—tracking brewery ingredients (malted barley for beer) across more than three years, showing multi-year institutional memory and auditing (Nissen, *Archaic Bookkeeping*).
- **Tamper-Proof Envelopes (Ur III & Old Babylonian):** Legal contracts and debt notes were written on small clay tablets, baked or dried, wrapped inside a thin outer **clay envelope** inscribed with a summary of the deal, and rolled with witnesses' cylinder seals. If anyone disputed the debt later, judges cracked open the sealed outer envelope in court to read the untouched inner tablet (Podany, *Weavers*).

## 4. Resource Scarcity, Trade & Mudbrick Engineering
- **What Sumer Lacked:** The southern alluvial plain had **almost no building stone, no copper or tin ore, and no tall structural timber** (local date palms have fibrous, spongy trunks poor for wide load-bearing roof beams). Everything other than mud, reeds, bitumen (natural asphalt seepage), grain, and sheep's wool had to be imported—cedar from Lebanon/Amanus mountains, copper from Oman (Magan), lapis lazuli from Afghanistan—paid for predominantly with exported **woven wool textiles** produced in workshops employing hundreds of women (Podany; Van De Mieroop).
- **Monumental Mudbrick & Bitumen:** Temples and ziggurats (such as the **White Temple on Anu Ziggurat at Uruk** and the later **Ziggurat of Ur** built under Ur-Nammu, c. 2100 BCE, measuring ~64 × 45 meters at the base) used millions of sun-dried mudbricks for the solid core (laid interspersed with woven reed mats and cables to absorb moisture and prevent cracking/shearing), encased in a weatherproof outer skin (~2.4 meters thick at Ur) of **kiln-baked bricks** set in waterproof **bitumen mortar** with weep-holes to drain rainwater so the mud core would not melt (Van De Mieroop).
